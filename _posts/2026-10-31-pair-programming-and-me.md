### Happy Halloween!

It's spooky season and I am avoiding chores by writing this blog post. I have been neglecting this site since being able to access my LinkedIn account again, which is a shame! Unfortunately, I am not predisposed to sharing my thoughts on the open internet. Usually I consider this a strength, but it does not make for engaging website content. However, my new job has given me lots of things to think about. Things I even feel like sharing!

Early this year I started a new job at Menlo Innovations, which is a software consultant firm that makes custom software solutions for a variety of clients. What's especially interesting to me is that the whole company operates on "pair programming" - a practice I had heard about but had never seen in the wild.

### Are you telling me a pair wrote this program?

Pair programming is when two programmers share a keyboard and mouse. One person "drives" and the other "navigates". At first blush it seems a bit wasteful to pay for twice the devs on every task, but as time as gone on I do see the benefit of it.

First benefit I have felt is that I actually get to engage with my colleagues! Much of my previous jobs involved being sent off to my own little corner, only to return upon completion of the task or if I needed something. My fellow engineers were always within reach, but it carried the silent burden of taking somebody off their work to focus on you. Now I always have somebody actively working *with* me on the same task. This exposes gaps in knowledge faster, transfers tribal knowledge from one person to the next, and encourages constant exchanging of ideas. When I get stuck or frustrated, I am not left to wallow on my own. Instead, I have somebody struggling through it with me!

Pair programming also speeds up onboarding. This is especially helpful in an environment like Menlo where you can be bouncing around between a bunch of projects with different tech stacks and client relationships. I didn't know shit about Salesforce before this job, and boy do I know a whole lot of it now. Usually when onboarding I would be consigned to walking through an old wiki before being unleashed upon the project. Now I get to ramp up and start work within the day I'm assigned to a project.

You are *constantly* communicating. This is not just the communication of the code you are writing but why you are writing this code, whether it achieves what the card asked for, if there's something more efficient to try, etc. I get to experience so many new ideas and ways of seeing that I would normally only get to see in carefully prepared meetings. This also extends to employee-employee feedback. You're encouraged to discuss "what you're working on" as far as self-improvement, and to figure out ways to get that improvement out of your time together.

Reflecting on my past few months pair programming, I would say the biggest effect this has had on me has been my mood! I'm a lot happier getting to work this way with my colleagues. It reminds me a lot of doing group projects in college, or working as a mentor for computer science students. I had been struggling with a sense of hopelessness that I could ever recapture that joy and enthusiasm - until now!

### The downsides
As with any strategy, there are tradeoffs. First among them being that engaging in that way for the whole day is EXHAUSTING. My first few months of pair programming involved me immediately going home and falling asleep after work. Lots of computer people are introverts, which means limited social battery - and I am no exception! I've noticed I've retreated from other social commitments so that I can have some amount of recovery time.

Also... sometimes there's work that's just easier to do solo. If you want to write a script or update a wiki or some other mundane task, it becomes more difficult to take that initiative when it involves dragging another person along. This is especially true if both of you are learning a brand new thing. Two people watching a YouTube tutorial or reading an article together feels a bit silly. In cases like this I think it'd be nice to go off on my own and return when I've learned enough to engage in the conversation.

### Final thoughts
I'm a bit surprised companies like this even exist. I'd gone from hearing about it in the abstract to learning that Ann Arbor has two companies who actively champion this process (the other one being Atomic Object). I'm so so grateful I get to be in an environment that encourages learning in this way. This is the most enthusiastic I've felt about being in tech since I began my career. Which is saying quite a bit, considering how bleak things tend to feel at this juncture.

I'm excited to learn more and post my findings here. May there be many new blog posts to come!
